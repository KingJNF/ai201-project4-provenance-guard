# Provenance Guard

A backend system that classifies whether submitted text-based creative work was written by a human or generated by AI, scores its confidence in that judgment, attaches a plain-language transparency label, and lets creators appeal a
classification they believe is wrong. Built around the principle that on a writing platform, **falsely accusing a human of using AI is worse than missing AI-generated text**. So the system is deliberately biased toward caution.

## What It Does

A creator submits a piece of writing. Provenance Guard runs it through an **ensemble of three independent detection signals**, combines them into a single calibrated confidence score, maps that score to one of three attribution
categories (`likely_ai`, `uncertain`, `likely_human`), and returns a plain-language transparency label. Every decision is recorded in a structured audit log, creators can appeal any classification, and a moderator-facing
analytics endpoint summarizes platform-wide detection patterns.

---

## Tech Stack

- **Language:** Python 3.11+
- **Web framework:** Flask 3.x
- **LLM provider:** Groq API — `llama-3.3-70b-versatile`
- **Rate limiting:** Flask-Limiter
- **Storage / audit log:** SQLite (Python standard library)
- **Config:** python-dotenv (`.env` for the Groq API key)

## Detection Signals

Provenance Guard uses three independent signals that measure different properties of the text. Their blind spots differ, so combining them is more informative than any single signal.

Signal 1: LLM Classifier (semantic)
Measures: Holistic semantic and stylistic coherence via Groq (llama-3.3-70b-versatile) such as naturalness of voice, specificity of detail, originality of phrasing, and presence of generic "AI-sounding" transitions. Returns a 0–1 AI-likelihood plus a one-sentence rationale.
Blind spot: Can be fooled by lightly-edited AI output and by formal, polished human writing (academic/technical), which shares surface features with AI text.

Signal 2: Stylometric Analyzer (structural)
Measures: Quantifiable structural variability such as sentence-length variance, type-token ratio (vocabulary diversity), and punctuation density/variety. High uniformity reads as more AI-like.
Blind spot: Blind to meaning entirely. Formal human registers score AI-like; casually-prompted AI scores human-like. Unreliable on short texts.

Signal 3: Repetition / Burstiness Heuristic (structural)
Measures: Repeated bigrams and repeated sentence-opening words. AI text tends to reuse phrasing and openers; human writing varies more.
Design note: Uses a neutral baseline (0.5). As a result, the absence of repetition is not treated as evidence of human authorship, so the signal abstains unless it actually detects repetition.


## Confidence Scoring & How It Was Validated

The three signals combine into a single AI-likelihood via a weighted average:

combined = (0.70 × llm) + (0.15 × stylometric) + (0.15 × repetition)

The LLM is weighted highest because calibration testing showed it to be the most reliable judge across the widest range of inputs; the two structural signals share the remaining weight as secondary checks.
The combined score maps to an attribution category using asymmetric thresholds. It takes more evidence to accuse AI than to clear a human:

| Combined AI-likelihood | Attribution   | User-facing confidence              |
|------------------------|---------------|-------------------------------------|
| >= 0.75                | likely_ai     | the combined score                  |
| 0.40 – 0.749           | uncertain     | peaks at 0.5 (maximum uncertainty)  |
| < 0.40                 | likely_human  | 1 - combined                        |


Why asymmetric? A false positive (labeling a human's work as AI), is more harmful on a writing platform than a false negative. The high 0.75 bar to accuse, paired with a wide uncertain band, deliberately biases the system away from false accusations.

Validation
I tested four inputs spanning the confidence range: clear AI, clear human, a formal human essay, and lightly-edited AI. Clear AI and clear human came out correctly with a wide confidence gap. The borderline cases (formal human, edited AI) correctly landed in uncertain / low-confidence bands rather than a confident verdict which is exactly the cautious behavior the design targets.

## Example Submissions

High-confidence example: clear human writing:
Input: A casual, conversational restaurant review with varied sentence lengths and informal punctuation.
Result: likely_human, confidence 0.8295 (83%)
Signals: llm 0.1, stylometric 0.1697, repetition 0.50, combined 0.1705
All three signals agree the text is human; high confidence.

Lower-confidence example: formal human writing (the hard case):
Input: A formal, academic paragraph on monetary policy and asset prices.
Result: uncertain, confidence: low (combined 0.708)
Signals: llm 0.8, stylometric 0.4868, repetition 0.50, combined 0.708
The formal register pushes the score toward AI (combined 0.708), but it stays just under the 0.75 accusation threshold. So the system says "Origin unclear" instead of falsely accusing a human writer. This is the asymmetric-threshold design working as intended.

## Transparency Labels

Three plain-language variants, shown to readers. The confidence percentage is embedded so the text visibly differs between high- and low-confidence results.

🤖 Likely AI-generated: "...probably created with the help of an AI tool (confidence: moderate, 76%). This is an automated estimate, not a certainty — the creator can request a review if they disagree."

✍️ Likely human-written: "...probably written by a person (confidence: high, 83%). This is an automated estimate, not a guarantee."

❓ Origin unclear: "We couldn't confidently determine whether this text was written by a person or generated by AI (confidence: low). Please treat the origin as unknown. Human authorship is entirely plausible."

The uncertain label is deliberately non-accusatory. It never tells a reader the text is AI.


## Appeals Workflow

A creator who disagrees with a classification can contest it. POST /appeal with the content_id and free-text reasoning:

Invoke-RestMethod -Uri "http://localhost:5000/appeal" -Method Post -ContentType "application/json" -Body '{"content_id": "<id>", "creator_reasoning": "I wrote this myself; I am a non-native English speaker and my style is formal."}'

On receipt, the system: (1) looks up the original classification, (2) flips its status to under_review, (3) logs the appeal — with the creator's reasoning and the original signal scores — directly beside the original decision in the audit log, and (4) returns a confirmation. No automatic re-classification occurs; a human moderator reviews the queue.

## Rate Limiting

POST /submit is limited to 10 requests per minute and 100 per day per client (via Flask-Limiter). Over the limit, the endpoint returns 429 Too Many Requests.

Reasoning: A genuine creator submits one piece at a time and reviews the result before submitting again, so 10/minute gives comfortable headroom for legitimate use (including quick re-submissions after edits) while stopping a script from flooding the endpoint. This is important because each submission triggers a paid LLM call. The 100/day cap controls sustained abuse and API cost while staying far above any individual writer's realistic daily need.

Verified: A 12-request burst returned 200 for the first 10 and 429 for the final 2.

## Audit Log

Every classification and appeal is recorded in SQLite with a full structured entry: content_id, creator_id, ISO timestamp, event_type, attribution, confidence, all individual signal scores, the LLM rationale, status, and (for appeals) the creator's reasoning. View it via GET /log:

Invoke-RestMethod -Uri "http://localhost:5000/log" -Method Get | ConvertTo-Json -Depth 5


## Stretch Features Implemented

1. Ensemble Detection:

Adds a third signal (repetition/burstiness) to the original two, combined via a documented weighted vote (0.70 / 0.15 / 0.15). Conflict resolution uses a neutral-baseline design: a non-discriminating structural signal abstains (stays ~0.5) rather than diluting the reliable semantic signal. All three individual scores are returned alongside the ensemble result in every /submit response and audit entry.

2. Analytics Dashboard:

A read-only GET /analytics endpoint aggregating the audit log into platform-wide metrics:

Invoke-RestMethod -Uri "http://localhost:5000/analytics" -Method Get | ConvertTo-Json -Depth 5

Returns: total classifications, verdict distribution (likely_ai / likely_human / uncertain), AI-to-human ratio, appeal count and appeal rate, and average confidence. In testing across 29 classifications, ~69% landed in uncertain which is the measurable footprint of the system's false-positive-avoidance design.


## Known Limitations

* The stylometric signal misreads polished, vocabulary-rich AI text as human. During calibration, a clearly-AI sample initially scored only 0.31 on this signal because it used varied vocabulary and punctuation, briefly pulling the combined verdict into uncertain.

* AI detection is inherently unreliable, especially for formal and non-native writing. This is a well-documented industry-wide problem. Detectors frequently flag human-written text (particularly formal or academic prose) as AI. Provenance Guard mitigates but cannot eliminate this. It should never be used as the sole basis to penalize a creator, which is exactly why the appeals workflow exists.

* Short texts are unreliable. Both structural signals require enough sentences/words to be meaningful and abstain (return ~0.5) below ~20 words.

* The LLM signal is non-deterministic at the margins and adds API latency.

* No authentication. creator_id is self-reported; in production this would require real authentication.

## Divergence from the Plan

The planning.md written before implementation specified a two-signal pipeline weighted 0.65 LLM / 0.35 stylometric. During calibration, I found this under-served strong LLM signals: a clearly-AI sample landed in uncertain because the stylometric signal dragged the combined score below the 0.75 threshold. I diverged in two ways:

1. Reweighted toward the LLM (the empirically more reliable signal), which moved clear AI correctly to likely_ai while keeping formal human text in uncertain without lowering the threshold, which would have created false positives.

2. Added a third signal (repetition) as the Ensemble stretch feature, with a neutral-baseline voting rule, settling on final weights of 0.70 / 0.15 / 0.15.

planning.md was intentionally left unchanged for the original weights to preserve the integrity of "planning before code". This section documents the empirically-driven evolution.
